# Fragmented Berkeley events create Lo-Cal's opening

**Yes, the need is real, but it is narrower than "students want another events website."** Berkeley's event supply is split by design. Departments publish on events.berkeley.edu, registered clubs use the login-walled CalLink, Haas uses CampusGroups and admits it has no central calendar, and most club, party and tech events live on Instagram, Luma and Partiful, which no official channel ingests. At least four student-built rivals are live, and each covers only one slice. The most active one, CalEvents, lists 1,840 events but only 12 from Luma, and none of them verifiably scrapes Instagram, Partiful, listservs or Slack at Lo-Cal's breadth. National survey data shows an online events calendar is the single most-wanted discovery aid. The same data, and the record at other campuses, shows that students act on events *pushed* to them (email digests, notifications, Instagram) and ignore portals they have to remember to visit. Precedents elsewhere also show that launching is easy and staying alive is hard: Instagram scraping breaks, maintainers graduate, and nobody publishes retention numbers. The verdict is to keep building Lo-Cal, but as a free push-and-subscribe layer with a clear owner after the founder leaves. The website should be the archive, not the product. One caveat matters: no Berkeley-specific student complaints or usage data were collected, so the demand case rests on structure and national data rather than measured Berkeley behavior.

## Four official systems split Berkeley events and none joins them

The fragmentation is structural, not anecdotal. **events.berkeley.edu** is "the official calendar system for promoting events that are hosted across campus" ([Strategic Communications](https://strategiccommunications.berkeley.edu/resources/berkeley-events/)). Its page never mentions student organizations. Registered clubs instead use **CalLink**, "the online central hub for Registered Student Organizations" ([LEAD Center](https://lead.berkeley.edu/get-involved)), covering more than 1,200 RSOs by the fall 2026 Calapalooza count ([Calapalooza](https://lead.berkeley.edu/about/calapalooza/)). Haas runs a third system, CampusGroups ([Haas](https://haas.berkeley.edu/ewmba/student-experience/student-groups-clubs/)), and its own knowledge base states that **Haas has no centralized calendar**: listings sit on the separate sites of each program and center ([Haas KB](https://kb.haas.berkeley.edu/knowledge-base/find-out-about-events-and-schedules-at-haas)).

The campus also points students away from its own systems. Berkeley Life tells students that "a lot of clubs share important information on events and applications on their respective social media pages" ([Berkeley Life](https://life.berkeley.edu/dive-into-clubs-at-cal/)). Its "stay in touch" page lists separate Instagram accounts, a monthly roundup email and a Basic Needs newsletter, each a separate follow or subscription ([Berkeley Life](https://life.berkeley.edu/how-to-stay-in-touch/)). The one official event app has a listing that dates to around 2019 and few ratings ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/uc-berkeley-cal-event-guides/id1004743884)).

Two technical facts shape the opportunity. First, events.berkeley.edu runs on LiveWhale and is easy to scrape. A third-party aggregator pulled **2,791 raw / 1,448 deduplicated upcoming events** from it in one run ([cal-events.com status](https://cal-events.com/status.json)). Second, an unauthenticated request to CalLink's discovery API redirected to a Campus Labs login page. That observation could reflect bot-blocking rather than a deliberate wall, but either way RSO events are hard for outsiders to aggregate. Clubs that never register through OASIS, such as Instagram-only groups, are absent from CalLink by construction ([LEAD Center](https://lead.berkeley.edu/get-involved)). That leaves a gap no official system is designed to fill: Instagram clubs, Luma and Partiful events, off-campus parties, and listserv and Slack announcements. Lo-Cal's source list targets exactly that gap.

## Four student-built rivals each cover one slice

The field is neither empty nor solved. Several Berkeley aggregators are live at once, which shows two things: other students feel the pain, and building a first version is cheap. None publishes traffic or user numbers.

| Product | What it covers | What it misses | Status signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CalEvents** (cal-events.com) | Official calendars: LiveWhale 1,448, BAMPFA 133, Cal Bears 131, Law, Haas, Simons; refreshed daily; exposes `events.json` and `llms-full.txt` | Only **12 Luma** events; no Instagram, CalLink, Partiful or parties | Verified refreshing, 1,840 events on 2026-11-02 ([status.json](https://cal-events.com/status.json)) |
| **Berkeley Events** (calevents.app) | Claims to parse "all of Berkeley's clubs", "1000+ Clubs" | Sources undisclosed | Page rendered only placeholders; freshness unverified ([calevents.app](https://www.calevents.app/)) |
| **Berkeley Goggles** | 1,300+ AI-summarized club profiles; an ASUC Office of the CTO project | Mainly a club directory; events section showed "Loading events…" | Live ([berkeleygoggles.org](https://berkeleygoggles.org/)) |
| **Eventreels** | Greek life, parties, tailgates, cultural socials, with RSVPs and tickets | Hosts must post there; requires a berkeley.edu login | Multi-campus, no Berkeley numbers ([Eventreels](https://eventreels.com/ucberkley)) |
| **berkeley-free-food-finder** | Crowd-posted free food | Early skeleton, 0 stars, no pushes since 2026-10-09 | Effectively a prototype ([GitHub](https://github.com/Shri2007/berkeley-free-food-finder)) |

Beyond campus, tech and hackathon events cluster on Luma. Berkeley clubs host hackathons there directly, from HKN's "Build Smth AI-Native Hackathon @Cal" to the Haas AI Club's OpenAI Codex Hackathon ([Luma](https://luma.com/7i8crpqz); [Luma](https://luma.com/laqw076z)). Cerebral Valley's digest, Partiful and Eventbrite are Bay Area-wide, so students still cross-reference them by hand ([Cerebral Valley](https://cerebralvalley.beehiiv.com/p/cerebral-valley-week-june-19th); [Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.com/d/ca--berkeley/uc-berkeley/)). For off-campus city events, Berkeleyside's calendar and Thursday "The Scene" roundup remain the local reference ([Berkeleyside](https://www.berkeleyside.org/events)).

Lo-Cal's defensible difference is breadth of the informal tier: about 850 Instagram accounts, Luma at scale, Partiful, hackathon sites, listservs and Slack. CalEvents already covers the official tier, so competing on official calendars adds little. Two risks follow from the table. Naming is crowded: "CalEvents" and "Berkeley Events (calevents.app)" are already near-identical, and Lo-Cal currently lives at a `bearly` URL that does not match its own name. And because no rival publishes usage, no one has proven a durable audience. The field is open, but retention is also unproven.

## Students want a calendar pushed to them, not another place to check

The strongest demand evidence is national, not Berkeley-specific. In an Inside Higher Ed / College Pulse survey of **1,250 students at 60 institutions** (November 2023), about a third chose an online campus events calendar as the aid that would most help them learn about events. That was the top pick, ahead of an in-app calendar, push notifications and specific-event emails, each chosen by a quarter or slightly more. Among students dissatisfied with their involvement, the order flipped: **push notifications 30%, online calendar 27%** ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/131056)). That subgroup is the one Lo-Cal most needs to reach, and it ranks being notified above browsing.

The belonging case gives the problem stakes. In the same survey family, students spending zero hours a week on activities reported 42% satisfaction with involvement, against 77–91% for those spending one or more hours. Ease of making friends rose from 37% to 56–61% ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/131056)). Gallup found 39% of bachelor's students felt lonely the previous day. That figure is reported secondhand ([Breitbart summarizing Gallup](https://www.breitbart.com/health/2023/08/11/nearly-4-10-college-students-report-feelings-loneliness)). Berkeley itself treats belonging as a live concern: more than 90% of Berkeley Connect participants say the program increased it ([CalParents](https://calparents.berkeley.edu/news/berkeley-connect-encourages-undergrads-%E2%80%9Cyou-belong-here%E2%80%9D)). These figures are correlations. Better discovery has not been shown to cause involvement.

The counter-signals are substantial and should shape the product rather than be dismissed. Word of mouth leads discovery in several studies: 57.1% at Ohio State for sports events ([OSU](https://cssl.osu.edu/posts/632320bc-704d-4eef-8bcb-87c83019f2e9/documents/2019-sls-marketing-report-final-accessible.pdf)), and face-to-face contact ranked first at Kenyon ([Kenyon](https://www.kenyon.edu/files/resources/report-communication-042815.pdf)). Only **3.11%** of Utah students used "online communities" to find events ([Utah NASPA](https://studentaffairs.utah.edu/assessment/_resources/documents/assessment-results-archive/aer/naspa-campus-activities-findings-and-actions.pdf)). In the IHE survey, "very few" students rated club social accounts as helpful ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/131056)). That suggests students value the *output* of Instagram aggregation more than more Instagram. Campus apps have a record of low adoption, though that evidence comes mostly from vendors with something to sell ([Ready Education](https://resources.readyeducation.com/blog/why-mobile-apps-fail)). NASPA data reported secondhand shows interest in events does not convert into attendance ([Insight into Academia](https://insightintoacademia.com/?p=39915)), and Chico State students said they were reluctant to attend alone ([CSU Chico](https://www.csuchico.edu/fye/_assets/documents/team5.pdf)). Awareness is necessary but not sufficient.

The audience is still large. Berkeley enrolled **33,122 undergraduates and 13,029 graduate students** in fall 2025 ([OPA](https://opa.berkeley.edu/campus-data/uc-berkeley-quick-facts)), with over 1,000 registered groups ([berkeley.edu](https://berkeley.edu/life/org.shtml)). The biggest gap in this research is local voice. Reddit refused automated fetches, and no Daily Cal coverage of discovery pain turned up. Absence of quotes here is a collection failure, not evidence of contentment.

## Other campuses show launches are easy and survival is hard

The clearest natural experiment comes from Saginaw Valley State. The school used Corq as its central event calendar for six years, until "the company that supported Corq went out of business". Its replacement sat behind MFA inside a portal. RSO leaders then reported **turnout down by about half** at some events and said "no one" they knew used the new calendar. What students did use was Instagram and a weekly "Event Blast" email with roughly a **60% open rate** ([Valley Vanguard, 2026-03-20](https://thevalleyvanguard.com/2026/03/20/after-corq-rsos-struggle-to-promote-events)). The case cuts both ways. Losing a single feed hurt attendance, so aggregation matters. But the login-gated portal failed, so the delivery format matters as much as the data.

Recent student aggregators follow a common arc. UIUC's eventsatuiuc.com, launched in April 2026, monitors **900+ RSO Instagram accounts** with AI classification and drew 1,500+ visitors in about ten days. Its founder had to run a fake account to get past Instagram's bot protection and was still updating it by hand daily a month later ([Daily Illini](https://dailyillini.com/news-stories/news-features/2026/05/05/ui-cs-student-creates-events-page-screen-time-app/)). Claremont's 5C Events launched with a Fizz post that got "thousands of likes" and reached 900+ Instagram followers in weeks. Its builders then struggled to get clubs to enter events themselves and to keep up with coursework ([The Student Life](https://tsl.news/new-5c-events-app-available-to-students-across-the-campuses/)). Swarthmore's Spotlyte got 400+ downloads in week one on host-submitted events only, and its status since is unknown ([Swarthmore Phoenix](https://swarthmorephoenix.com/2023/02/02/students-launch-mobile-app-to-spotlight-campus-social-scene%EF%BF%BC/)). None of these projects has published a year-one follow-up, which itself signals how they tend to end. UT Austin's Longhorn Loop is the structural exception: a student *club* runs it, with per-source scraper issues and a 95% capture target, so it does not depend on one founder ([GitHub](https://github.com/Longhorn-Developers/Longhorn-Loop)).

The most durable precedents are the least sophisticated. Free-food mailing lists have run for over a decade at MIT, Stanford and Princeton on crowd posting plus volunteer or student-government moderation. Princeton's list carried 900+ emails in one year ([Daily Princetonian](https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2015/11/listserv-analysis-shows-frist-murray-dodge-most-popular-places-for-free-food-distribution)). MIT's had to add moderation after spam ([MIT mailman](https://mailman.mit.edu/mailman/listinfo/free-foods)). At the commercial level, no venture-scale company has won on event discovery alone. Facebook Campus reached 204 schools and was shut down for lack of use ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/03/02/facebook-is-shutting-down-its-college-student-only-social-network-campus/amp)). Fizz grew to 620+ campuses through anonymous social and then commerce, not events ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/03/college-social-app-fizz-expands-into-grocery-delivery)). Anthology, which owned Engage and Corq, went through Chapter 11 in 2025 and sold off Engage ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/index%2ephp/experience/anthology-chapter-11-restructuring)). Partiful grew by making *hosting* better and pays campus ambassadors to recruit hosts ([Talentify](https://www.talentify.io/job/campus-growth-manager-spring-2026-myrtle-point-oregon-us-partiful-af908f89-626d-4d26-a851-a39ba62737ff)). That supply is public, and an aggregator can ride it instead of competing with it. Together these cases argue that a free, open-source project is the right model, because no monetization path for student-side discovery has been documented.

## What Lo-Cal should do: push, specialize, and plan for succession

**Distribution should be push-first.** The website at the `bearly` URL should serve as the browsable archive, not the main product. The evidence points to four channels, in roughly this order.

1. **Subscribable ICS feeds by category** (tech/hackathons, free food, parties, talks), so events show up in the calendars students already check.
2. **A weekly email digest**, the format behind SVSU's 60% open rate.
3. **An Instagram account and free-food alerts**, meeting students where club supply already lives.
4. **Shareable per-event links** for group chats, because word of mouth dominates discovery.

For launch, the precedents point to Fizz and r/berkeley posts, timed to the start of a semester and to Calapalooza, when free-food and club volume peak. The highest-leverage single move is getting into an existing push channel the campus already owns: the monthly Berkeley Life Roundup reaches every student automatically ([Berkeley Life](https://life.berkeley.edu/how-to-stay-in-touch/)).

**Differentiation should lean on the informal tier and on curation.** Lo-Cal should not compete with CalEvents on official calendars. Its edge is what CalEvents lacks: Luma at scale, Partiful, Instagram, hackathons, listservs and Slack. If the bottleneck is overload rather than scarcity, filters are the core value, not a garnish. That means "free food today", "hackathons this month", deduplication and a strong default view. Free food deserves first-class treatment, since it is the one discovery use case with a decade of proven demand. Club directories (Goggles) and host platforms (Eventreels, Partiful) are complements Lo-Cal can link to, not rivals it has to beat. Lo-Cal should also publish its own usage and coverage numbers. No rival does, and events-per-week and subscriber counts would both prove the need and set Lo-Cal apart.

**Sustainability depends on planning for the founder's exit and on tiering sources by fragility.** Instagram is both the most distinctive source and the most fragile. UIUC's experience (a fake account, manual daily runs) is the likeliest failure mode for an 850-account scraper. Lo-Cal should therefore treat Luma, LiveWhale, Partiful and the campus feeds as the stable backbone, and Instagram as a degradable tier with health checks that flag silent breakage. On succession, the projects that last share three traits: an institutional or club owner (Longhorn Loop, the Princeton list's move to student government), low-infrastructure hosting, and no single-person manual step. That suggests documenting each scraper with a test harness, keeping the static GitHub Pages hosting, and courting a durable owner such as a CS club or the ASUC Office of the CTO, which already backs Goggles. Being open source is a real asset here: it is what lets the project outlive one maintainer, which no closed rival can offer.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "do students want an aggregated calendar?" to "will anyone keep it running and put it in front of students?" The demand is plausibly there: the supply gap is structural, a calendar is the top national ask, and SVSU showed that removing a central feed halves turnout. The failure modes, though, are about delivery and upkeep, not data. Lo-Cal's scrapers already solve the hard supply problem that sank submission-based apps like 5C Events and Spotlyte. What is unproven is whether Berkeley students will subscribe and keep subscribing, and whether the Instagram tier survives a semester of bot protection.

The verdict is **yes, there is a real need, and Lo-Cal is better positioned than any current Berkeley rival to meet it**, on the condition that it becomes a feed rather than a destination. The cheapest way to retire the remaining uncertainty is for Lo-Cal to produce the Berkeley evidence this research could not find, within one semester: ICS and digest subscriber counts, weekly event volume by source, and a manual sweep of r/berkeley for discovery complaints. If subscriptions stay flat after a well-timed launch, the counter-signals (word of mouth wins, interest does not become attendance) were right. If they grow, Lo-Cal has something no Berkeley aggregator has shown so far: a measured audience.
